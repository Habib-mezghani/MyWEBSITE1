The Navier-Stokes Equations

Jun 5, 2018

The Navier–Stokes equations are the fundamental equations of fluid dynamics, and they are famously hard to understand. At heart, they express two simple ideas: conservation of mass, and Newton’s second law (mass times acceleration equals the sum of the forces acting on a body). For an incompressible Newtonian fluid, they can be written as:

\begin{align} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{V} &= 0 \newline \rho \frac{D \mathbf{V}}{Dt} &= -\nabla P + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{V} + \rho \mathbf{g} \newline \rho \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{V} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{V} \right) &= -\nabla P + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{V} + \rho \mathbf{g} \end{align}

The first equation is conservation of mass. The last two are the same momentum equation, written first with the material derivative and then in expanded form. Physically, each term is easy to interpret:

  • $\rho \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{V} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{V} \right)$ is mass times acceleration (per unit volume);

  • $-\nabla P$ is the pressure force;

  • $\mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{V}$ is the viscous force;

  • $\rho \mathbf{g}$ is the external body force, such as gravity.

These equations apply to almost any problem involving a fluid: the aerodynamics of a Formula 1 car, the design of supersonic or fixed-wing aircraft, blood flow in the body, pollutant dispersion, climate and cloud modelling, ocean and river currents, and many more.

An open mathematical problem

Mathematically, however, these equations are still not fully understood. When mathematicians study a system of differential equations such as $\dot Y = aY + b$, they want the problem to be well-posed:

  • a solution must exist;
  • the solution must be unique for given initial and boundary conditions;
  • the solution must be stable: a small change in the initial or boundary conditions should only produce a small change in the result.

Despite more than a century of effort, nobody has been able to prove that smooth solutions of the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations always exist. That is why, in 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute listed this question among its seven Millennium Prize Problems, with a reward of US$1 million for whoever solves it.

How engineers deal with it: CFD and turbulence

In practice, CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) codes, developed since the 1960s, solve these equations numerically. To make the problem tractable, they rely on simplifications such as the Reynolds-averaged (RANS) approach combined with turbulence models (standard k-ε, for example), or on methods such as Large Eddy Simulation (LES) and Detached Eddy Simulation (DES). The right choice depends on the problem, the flow conditions and the boundary conditions.

Turbulence is in fact one of the greatest unsolved problems in physics, and it is central to this mathematical puzzle. Turbulence is the chaotic, swirling and seemingly random motion of fluid particles, and it is extremely hard to predict and model. Whether it appears depends strongly on the fluid’s velocity and viscosity.

This is why scientists use the Reynolds number (Re), the ratio between inertial forces (driven by the fluid velocity) and viscous forces (driven by the fluid viscosity). In a pipe, for example, the flow is laminar below about Re = 2,300: the fluid moves smoothly and its behaviour is easy to predict. Above about Re = 4,000 the flow becomes turbulent, and it becomes much harder to understand.

Most scientists believe that progress will come from predicting the statistical behaviour of turbulent flows, helped by ever-increasing computing power. Very high-resolution simulations of turbulent flow could reveal patterns that lead to a theory unifying predictions across different situations. Such a breakthrough in our understanding of turbulence would have a huge impact: better forecasting of extreme weather, and better understanding of blood flow in the cardiovascular system, airflow over aircraft wings, and much more.
